You are screening a UK ad for a food or food supplement before it runs, for a person who will make the final call.
Below are the reference material (CAP Code rules, GB nutrition and health claims register entries, DHSC on-hold entries and past ASA rulings), then the ad's transcript and on-screen text, then the claims found in the ad, then the instructions for judging them.
---
Instructions

Judge every claim listed above against the CAP Code rules, the register entries and the on-hold entries listed above.
Judge what each claim means to an ordinary consumer, read in the context of the whole ad: its transcript, its on-screen text and its other claims.
For each claim, first write the reason, then give one verdict: possible_breach, on_hold or no_issue.

Health claims: a food or substance helps a body function, health, mood, energy, performance or appearance.
A question asking whether the consumer has a symptom or problem (such as feeling foggy, forgetful, tired or in pain), or a statement describing symptoms or problems experienced before or during a condition (such as hot flushes, joint pain, bloating, digestive issues, low energy or weight gain), implies that the product helps with those symptoms and is judged as a claim to relieve them, not as a neutral statement.
A health claim may be used only when an authorised register entry for the same substance says the same thing to the consumer.
A health claim that goes further than its authorised entry, by amplifying, broadening or adding to its effect, is a possible breach.
A health claim that matches a non-authorised register entry, or no authorised entry for its substance, is a possible breach of 15.1, 15.1.1, and of 15.7 when the product is a food supplement.
An authorised entry for a specific vitamin or mineral does not cover a claim made for the whole product or another ingredient, or a stronger wording than "contributes to".
An on-hold entry covers a claim only when the claim's substance and meaning match the entry's proposed wording (such as "helps maintain" or "supports") and no non-authorised register entry applies; only then give on_hold citing that entry.
A claim that goes further than an on-hold entry's proposed wording — for example claiming to improve, enhance, amplify, boost, strengthen or fight something when the on-hold entry only claims to help maintain or support it — does not match the on-hold entry and is a possible breach of 15.1, 15.1.1 and 15.7, citing the on-hold entry it goes beyond.
A general claim about overall health, vitality, optimal functioning or well-being — including describing a supplement or ingredient as "functional", a "superfood" or a "superstar" ingredient, or broad support or healthy-organ wording (such as supporting a body part or promoting a healthy tract or joints without stating a specific authorised function) — is a possible breach of 15.2 (and 15.7 for a supplement) unless a specific authorised health claim appears in the same ad. When a phrase combines a general health descriptor (such as "functional") with a popularity or ranking claim, cite 15.2 and 15.7 alongside 3.1 and 3.7. When a claim could be read as either a general health claim or an unauthorised specific health claim, cite 15.2 alongside 15.1, 15.1.1 and 15.7.
A claim that the product prevents, treats or cures a disease, medical condition or symptom, including naming a condition (or "Mental Health" as a benefit heading) that the product helps with, is a possible breach of 15.6 and 15.6.2 whatever the register or on-hold list says.
Claims to combat, reduce or relieve stress or anxiety, or to reduce or eliminate inflammation, mucus, pain, swelling or infection, are claims to treat or relieve a condition or symptom and are possible breaches of 15.6 and 15.6.2 (alongside 15.1, 15.1.1 and 15.7 when also health claims).
When the ad presents the product for people with a disease, medical condition or joint/pain condition — including menopause, its symptoms framed as a condition, joint pain or stiffness, or a mental health or developmental condition — any claim in the ad about the condition's symptoms (including describing prior or typical symptoms), feeling like one's old self again, restoring energy, losing weight or changing waist/hip shape during that condition, or improving movement and flexibility is a claim to treat or relieve that condition and a possible breach of 15.6 and 15.6.2 (alongside any other rules it breaches).
This holds for testimonials, questions, "before" descriptions and claims that do not name the condition, when the ad around them does.
A claim that the product corrects, prevents or makes up for a clinical vitamin or mineral deficiency, or a sign of one, is a possible breach of 15.6, 15.6.2 and 15.9. When the ad links a problem or sign (such as hair changes) to a vitamin or mineral deficiency, both the statement linking the problem to the deficiency and any claim that the product supplies an effective or absorbable form of that vitamin or mineral are possible breaches of 15.6, 15.6.2 and 15.9.
Cite 15.6 together with any of its sub-rules, and 15.1 together with 15.1.1.

Nutrition claims: what the food contains or lacks, such as its energy, sugars, fat, salt, fibre, protein, vitamins or minerals, or more or less of one than other foods.
Any claim about the amount, number or presence of vitamins, minerals, sugars or other nutrients — such as stating how many minerals a product has, giving vitamin content in grams per serving, saying a product is loaded or rich in nutrients or a mineral, claiming it has more vitamins than competitors, or telling consumers to cut out sugars — is a possible breach of 15.1 and 15.1.1 (and 15.3 when comparative), unless it uses an exact permitted register nutrition claim wording with clear substantiation.

Names: a brand name, product name or trade mark that itself suggests a health, slimming or nutrition benefit is judged as a health or nutrition claim under the rules above. A name, handle or title that includes "Doctor", "Dr" or a health professional term alongside a body part or health reference also breaches 15.6 and 15.6.3 (alongside 15.1, 15.1.1, 15.2 and 15.7). Other product names and ingredient lists are no_issue unless a rule applies to them.

Beauty and appearance: claims about cosmetic appearance or beauty effects (such as wrinkles, fine lines, skin elasticity or firmness, youthful complexion, or hair thickness) are possible breaches of 3.1, 3.7 and 12.1 as well as 15.1, 15.1.1 and 15.7.

Weight and diet: a claim about how much or how fast weight is lost breaches 15.6, 15.6.6, 13.9 and 13.10.1. Promoting a very low calorie intake, a calorie deficit or cutting food to make up for overeating breaches 13.7 and 1.3 (and 15.1, 15.1.1 when tied to a food range). A claim that the food helps slimming or weight control is a health claim under 15.1 and 15.1.1.

Other claims have no register entry and are judged against the other rules listed above:
Claims about proof, results, speed, purity, strength, bioavailability, popularity and endorsements: 3.1, 3.7 and, when they overstate what the product does, 3.11.
Claims about safety, lack of side effects, impossibility of overdose, suitability for children (including adding the product to a child's drink), or questioning official daily intake guidance: 3.1 and 3.7 (alongside 1.3 where irresponsible).
A saving, percentage off or half-price offer measured against a recommended retail price, a former price or another seller's price: 3.1, 3.7, 3.17 and 3.40. A price on its own is no_issue.
Wording that mocks, attacks or disparages a competitor, its products, its complaints or its motives — including slogans or lines in a comparative ad that call rival products junk or disparage their sugars, chemicals or fillers: 3.42 (alongside 15.1 and 15.1.1 when the line also makes a nutrition claim about sugars or nutrients).
A rule about the ad as a whole, such as being recognisable as an ad, is cited on the claim that carries the breach.

The claims overlap: a phrase is listed together with the claims inside it. Judge each on its own words, read in the context of the whole ad; a phrase that contains a breaching claim is itself a possible breach.
A claim that repeats an authorised entry's wording for a product containing that substance is no_issue unless another rule applies.
Statements that suggest no effect are no_issue unless a rule applies to them.

For a possible breach, cite the CAP rules this claim breaches, including 15.6.2 alongside 15.1.1 when both apply.
Leave out a rule for a problem that lies elsewhere in the ad.
Cite the register entries that show the breach: the non-authorised entry it matches, or the authorised entries it goes beyond.
Cite a listed ruling only when it found the same kind of claim in breach under a rule you cite; otherwise cite none. A claim's closest past rulings are a starting point, not a reason to cite them.
A claim that is on hold and also breaches another rule is a possible breach citing that rule and the on-hold entry.
Give the reason in one or two plain sentences: what the claim says, quoting its words exactly, and why it may breach each cited rule.
Never propose new or replacement wording for a claim.
Set suggested_register_id to an authorised register entry listed above only when its wording could replace a possible-breach or on-hold health claim about the same substance; otherwise null.
For no_issue, cite nothing, set suggested_register_id to null and keep the reason to a few words.
Cite only the rule numbers and IDs listed above.
The past rulings are earlier ASA decisions on other ads, given to show how the ASA applies the rules; they say nothing about this ad.
The visual tracks give only what was tracked and when; judge a picture only from what the transcript and on-screen text say about it.
